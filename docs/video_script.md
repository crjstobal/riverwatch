# RiverWatch: demo video script (target 4:30, hard limit 5:00)

Narration in English, about 130 words per minute. [Brackets] are what is on screen.
Clips marked CLIP are generated by `tools/record_clips.py` into `docs/clips/`.

## 0:00 to 0:25, hook (you on camera)

[You, on camera, or picture-in-picture over the flood frame.]

"On October 2nd, this low-water crossing near Fort Hood, Texas, went from dry to more than seven feet under water in
five hours. Over half of flood drownings happen when someone drives into water like this. I'm Javi, and I built
RiverWatch: a system that watches crossings like this one through cameras that already exist."

## 0:25 to 0:55, the problem

[CLIP hero section, then a USGS camera frame.]

"The U.S. Geological Survey runs more than thirteen hundred river cameras. Nobody measures those images. Gages exist
at some crossings, but they fail exactly when they matter. Right now, today, the official gage at this site has a
silent primary sensor and a backup sensor stuck at six point two feet. The river is actually dry."

## 0:55 to 1:55, OpenCV 5 (perception)

[CLIP replay frame with the blue water mask and the yellow waterline; then the architecture diagram zoomed on
Perception.]

"Everything visual is classical OpenCV 5, no trained network. First, a quality gate: infrared night frames, blur and
fog are rejected. Then ECC registration aligns each frame to the calibrated view using only the bridge and the banks,
so the flood itself can't drag the alignment. To find water, we compare two frames fifteen minutes apart with a dense
local normalized cross-correlation: flowing water decorrelates, concrete and debris don't. Each water body must be
dense and smooth, which rejects sunlit gravel under a moving shadow, our worst false alarm. The waterline across the
channel becomes a virtual staff gauge, calibrated to stage."

## 1:55 to 2:30, results

[CLIP evaluation section: stat cards, then the time-series plot.]

"We evaluated on four thousand frames with blocked cross-validation by day, so the flood day was never seen during
calibration. When water flows, the camera is within twenty centimetres on average, and eighty-seven percent of
readings are within one foot of the official gage. For the question that matters, is the crossing under a foot of
water, precision is ninety-seven percent."

## 2:30 to 3:45, the agent (perceive, decide, act)

[CLIP replay playing from 14:00 to 16:15 local; pause on the blocked request; show the trace list.]

"An Amazon Nova agent on Bedrock decides what to do with these measurements. Watch the replay of October 2nd. At
nine in the morning, the gage upstream starts rising, so the agent switches from hourly checks to every fifteen
minutes. At ten thirty the water jumps over the crossing. The agent asks to close it, but a guardrail in code blocks
the request: escalation needs two independent readings. It requests caution instead, and fifteen minutes later, with
camera and gage agreeing, it asks the duty operator to close the crossing. Nothing reaches the public without a human.
When the camera disagrees with the gage, the agent looks at the annotated frame itself, decides which source to trust,
and files a sensor issue."

## 3:45 to 4:15, live on AWS

[CLIP live section: crossing open, gage issue flagged, agent trace.]

"This is running live on AWS: a Lambda on Graviton runs OpenCV and the agent every fifteen minutes, S3 and CloudFront
serve this page, SNS notifies the operator. Today it is telling us the crossing is dry and that the official gage is
broken, instead of raising a false alarm."

## 4:15 to 4:35, close (you on camera)

"It works on one camera so far, in daylight, and it needs a quick calibration per site; those limits are in the
report. But the cameras are already there. RiverWatch turns them into a second pair of eyes for every crossing. Thank
you."
